## Embedded Autonomy, State Capacity and Market Institutions
**Research Interest**
My research interest lies in the relationship between state capacity, market institutions and Evans's concept of _embedded autonomy_. I am particularly interested in whether China's Social Credit System (SCS) represents a new form of information-based state–business embeddedness, and whether digital governance can improve market coordination while preserving bureaucratic autonomy and avoiding political capture.

### 1. Acemoglu, García-Jimeno & Robinson (2015) — State Capacity and Economic Development: A Network Approach
_American Economic Review_, 105(8), 2364–2409. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20140044)
**Research Content**
Using Colombian municipal data and a network model of government investment, the authors demonstrate that state capacity exhibits **strategic complementarities** across jurisdictions. Strengthening one municipality's administrative capacity increases the returns to institutional investment in neighbouring municipalities, generating positive spillovers. Consequently, decentralised governments may underinvest because they fail to internalise these external benefits. The findings suggest that effective state capacity depends not only on individual institutions but also on coordination across government networks.

**Why Selected**
This paper highlights **institutional coordination** as a fundamental dimension of state capacity. It provides a useful framework for understanding SCS, which relies on cross-departmental information sharing and regulatory cooperation. It raises the possibility that SCS's effectiveness depends on complementarities between government agencies and local administrations, rather than information collection alone.

### 2. Aneja & Xu (2024) — Strengthening State Capacity: Civil Service Reform and Public Sector Performance during the Gilded Age
_American Economic Review_, 114(8), 2352–2387. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20230019)
**Research Content**
Using historical US postal records from 1875–1901, the authors examine how civil service reform affected public-sector performance. Under the political patronage system, elections frequently caused personnel turnover, disrupting administrative continuity and institutional knowledge. Civil service protections reduced politically motivated dismissals, allowing bureaucrats to accumulate experience and improve organisational efficiency. The study finds that reform reduced postal delivery errors and increased productivity, particularly during election years, demonstrating how **bureaucratic autonomy** can strengthen state capacity.

**Why Selected**
This paper directly relates to the **autonomy** component of Evans's _embedded autonomy_. It demonstrates that effective state capacity requires institutional independence from short-term political interference, rather than simply stronger administrative powers. Applied to SCS, it raises the question of whether improved information capacity can support impartial and consistent regulation, or whether political incentives may still produce selective enforcement.

### 3. Beraja, Yang & Yuchtman (2023) — Data-intensive Innovation and the State: Evidence from AI Firms in China
_Review of Economic Studies_, 90(4), 1701–1723. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdac056)
**Research Content**
The authors investigate how government-controlled data influence private-sector innovation in China's facial-recognition industry. Using firm-level data on public-security procurement contracts and a triple-differences approach, they find that firms receiving data-rich government contracts subsequently developed more commercial AI software. The mechanism operates through **government procurement → access to valuable data → algorithmic improvement → commercial innovation**. The findings demonstrate how state–firm relationships can generate productive information spillovers, while potentially reinforcing government surveillance capabilities.

**Why Selected**
This paper provides an empirical example of **information-based state–business embeddedness**, illustrating how government-controlled information can reshape private economic activity. It offers a conceptual bridge between Evans's traditional emphasis on productive state–business relationships and SCS as a potential form of digital governance. Unlike government procurement, SCS primarily collects and shares information _about firms_ for regulatory purposes. This distinction raises the question of whether digital information systems can strengthen productive state–market coordination without expanding administrative discretion or political dependence.
