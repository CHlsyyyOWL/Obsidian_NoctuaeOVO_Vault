## State Capacity and Market Institutions 

State capacity is not simply the government's ability to intervene. Its economic effects depend on institutional coordination, bureaucratic incentives, and how government policies alter firms' decisions and market equilibrium.

### 1. Acemoglu, García-Jimeno & Robinson (2015) State Capacity and Economic Development: A Network Approach
_American Economic Review_, 105(8), 2364–2409. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20140044)
**Background**
- State capacity varies substantially across municipalities, even within the same country.
- Local governments are economically and administratively interconnected. Weak institutions in one municipality can reduce the benefits of institutional investment elsewhere.

**Economic mechanism**
- Strategic complementarity: Increasing state capacity in municipality A raises the **marginal return** to capacity investment in municipality B.
- When neighbouring governments improve administration and public-service provision, complementary investments become more productive.
- Consequently, decentralised decisions may produce **underinvestment in state capacity**: each government neglects benefits accruing to other jurisdictions.
- Network effects amplify initial interventions because improvements in one municipality encourage further investments elsewhere.

**Methods**
- Colombian municipality-level data and a network model of local and central government investment.
- Historical variation, including colonial administrative presence and transportation networks, helps identify differences in institutional capacity and interconnectedness.
- Structural modelling incorporates governments' strategic responses and indirect spillovers.

**Findings**
- Substantial strategic complementarities between municipalities' capacity investments.
- Direct spillovers account for approximately 50% of the quantified effect of capacity expansion; equilibrium network responses amplify the overall impact.
- The economic returns to strengthening a municipality depend partly on the capabilities of other municipalities.

**Interpretation / limitations**
- State capacity is a networked institutional outcome, not merely an isolated stock of administrative resources.
- Results depend on the network structure and modelling assumptions.
- Focuses primarily on government-to-government interactions rather than direct state–firm relationships.

### 2. Aneja & Xu (2024) — Strengthening State Capacity: Civil Service Reform and Public Sector Performance during the Gilded Age
_American Economic Review_, 114(8), 2352–2387. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20230019)

**Background**
- Nineteenth-century US public administration operated partly through the political spoils system.
- Electoral turnover could trigger politically motivated dismissals and appointments, disrupting administrative continuity.
- Civil service reforms progressively protected positions from partisan interference.

**Economic mechanism**
- **Political patronage → personnel turnover → loss of institutional knowledge → lower productivity.**
- Political appointments prioritise loyalty rather than administrative competence and can weaken incentives to accumulate job-specific expertise.
- Employment protections reduce politically driven turnover, allowing organisational knowledge and experience to accumulate.
- **Key distinction:** Political control over bureaucrats is not equivalent to effective administrative capacity.

**Methods**
- Digitised US postal records covering 1875–1901.
- Exploits differences in the timing and coverage of civil service reforms.
- Examines postal delivery errors and productivity, including variation around elections.

**Findings**
- Civil service reform reduced postal delivery errors and increased productivity.
- Improvements were particularly pronounced during election years, consistent with reduced disruption from partisan personnel changes.
- Additional evidence suggests that reform may have weakened certain local political patronage networks.

**Interpretation / limitations**
- Professional bureaucratic autonomy can strengthen state capacity by insulating administration from short-term political incentives.
- Provides evidence on a specific public service rather than economy-wide growth.
- Does not directly establish how bureaucratic reform changes market institutions or private investment.

### 3. Barwick, Kalouptsidi & Zahur (2025) — Industrial Policy Implementation: Empirical Evidence from China's Shipbuilding Industry

_Review of Economic Studies_, 92(6), 3611–3648. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdaf011)

**Background**
- China provided extensive industrial support to shipbuilding, contributing to rapid capacity expansion and increased global market share.
- Industrial expansion can nevertheless coexist with overcapacity, fragmented production and inefficient capital allocation.

**Economic mechanisms**
- **Production subsidies:** Reduce effective marginal production costs, encouraging greater output.
- **Investment subsidies:** Lower capital costs, encouraging expansion of productive capacity.
- **Entry subsidies:** Reduce entry barriers, potentially attracting firms whose productivity would not justify entry without government support.
- Excessive entry increases fragmentation and can leave capacity underutilised; additional production also depresses international prices.
- **Policy success ≠ economic efficiency:** Achieving industrial scale or market-share targets does not establish that social benefits exceed policy costs.

**Methods**
- Chinese shipbuilding firm data, 1998–2014.
- Dynamic structural model of firms' entry, exit, investment and production decisions.
- Counterfactual simulations compare subsidy instruments, policy timing, duration and targeting.

**Findings**
- Industrial support substantially increased investment, firm entry and China's international market share.
- Overall policy returns were relatively low, with excess capacity, industry fragmentation and lower global ship prices.
- Entry subsidies generated particularly severe distortions.
- Better targeting, countercyclical implementation and shorter interventions could reduce inefficiencies.
- Lower ship prices may also generate indirect benefits through cheaper shipping and international trade.

**Interpretation / limitations**
- Government capacity to expand an industry does not guarantee efficient market outcomes.
- The design of incentives matters as much as the scale of intervention.
- Counterfactual conclusions depend on structural assumptions.
- The paper evaluates policy instruments rather than directly measuring bureaucratic competence or state–business embeddedness.


1. **Coordination:** State capacity depends on complementarities between institutions; fragmented investment can be inefficient.
2. **Incentives:** Stronger political control does not necessarily produce stronger administrative performance.
3. **Market allocation:** Greater intervention capacity does not necessarily produce higher welfare; outcomes depend on how policies alter private incentives.

Effective state–market relations require more than administrative power or close government–business connections. Internal bureaucratic organisation, intergovernmental coordination and market incentive compatibility may jointly determine whether state intervention supports development or generates distortions. These papers illuminate complementary components of this argument, but none directly tests Evans's embedded-autonomy framework.

Under what institutional conditions does greater state capacity complement market coordination and competition, rather than substitute for or distort market incentives?
