# The Legalization Trap — Spoken Script v2 (~13–14 minutes)

_LSE Knowledge Beyond Boundaries 2026 · Jiatong Liu_

Good afternoon, everyone. My name is Jiatong Liu. I am a second year student at UCL and have just finished my exchange in Sciencepo. Today I am very honorable to open the panel discussion.  

Today I want to talk about a common misunderstanding of the welfare state that what I called it the legalization trap. Which is syaing that a government can write welfare rights into law, and still fail to deliver them. My evidence comes from China's pension system, between 2015 and 2024 and a few other countries in the Asia-Pacific as the pattern is much wider than that. 

*next slide

So let me start with the numbers, because the numbers set up the whole puzzle.

In China, the basic rural pension is about 143 yuan a month. That is around 20 US dollars. To put it plainly, a whole month of pension buys you about five cups of coffee.

*next slide*
Now you might think that is just the poorest regions. So look at the spread. _[Show Chart 1 — provincial pension]_ One city, Shanghai, pays over 1490 yuan a month. That is more than ten times the national floor. Beijing is second, at around 960. But **outside a handful of rich coastal places,** most of the country sits right at the floor, around 143. The national average looks like 223. But that average lies, because a few rich cities pull it up while most provinces cluster at the bottom.

*next slide*
or we can make it more  simpler. _[Show Chart 3 — tiers]_ China runs its own poverty line, called dibao. 
In 2024 the rural dibao averaged about 594 yuan a month. So the state itself says you need around 594 yuan just to get by. But the base pension is only 143. That is less than a quarter of the government's own floor. And a retired civil servant gets about 6,000 yuan a month. 

*next slide*
one might ask, why government cannot guarantee more pension funds? or we ask, where the money went?
In 2024, official audits found that 13 provinces had quietly moved 40.6 billion yuan out of the resident pension fund. They used it to pay government salaries, to keep local offices running, and to repay debt. To give you a sense of scale, that is an amount equal to the full yearly base pension of about 24 million rural pensioners.

*next slide*
OK, although we have little pensions for 180 million rural pensioners, but old people can lean on their children. But here is the problem with that. About 600 million people in China earn around 10,000 yuan a year. That is close to 40 percent of the whole population. For most of them, one serious illness already means ruin. poor pensions which made elderly people must lean on their children to maintain lving standard only adds on the financial burden of the whole household.

And you might ask, what about health insurance. Here the two problems share one root. Pensions and health subsidies both depend on local government money. So when the local budget breaks, both get squeezed at once. It is not one program failing. It is the whole local welfare base eroding together.

So on paper the constitution guarantees protection. But the numbers tell a very different story.  This is not a simple problem regarding state financial capacity or economic behaviour, but is what I want to explain.

*next slide*
And before everything I need to point to a problem in how we usually study welfare. . We tend to treat the written law as if it were the reality on the ground. But the two are not the same thing.

Think about the two scholars who shaped this whole field. Esping-Andersen, back in 1990, sorted welfare states by reading what the law says. Coverage rules, replacement rates, who qualifies on paper. Then Pierson, in 1994, measured welfare cuts the same way. For him, if the law did not change, then no cut had happened.

Both of them lean on one assumption. They assume the written law is a good stand-in for what the state actually does. And for rich democracies, with strong courts and steady budgets, that assumption mostly holds. The law and the delivery move together.

But my argument is that the assumption breaks down. And it does not break down at random. It breaks down for a reason, and under specific conditions. 

---

### [Slide 4a — The Motive]

**First the reason. Why would a state write a right it does not intend to delive**r. Because the writing and the delivery do two different jobs.

Writing the right buys legitimacy. It makes the state look modern, caring, and bound by law. But actually enforcing the right does the opposite of what the state wants. Enforcement ties its own hands. It turns a flexible promise into a fixed obligation. So the state keeps the promise vague on purpose. Vague promises preserve discretion, and discretion is power. In other words, the gap between the law and the delivery is not a flaw in the system. For the state, it is the point.

---

### [Slide 4b — The Three Conditions]

That is the motive. But a motive is not enough. The gap also needs structure to survive, and to stay hidden. So here are three structural conditions that hold it open.

The first condition is reach. A central government can write a law in the capital, but it cannot enforce that law everywhere by itself. It has to rely on local agents, and it cannot fully watch them. Michael Mann called this the limit of a state's infrastructural power, meaning the power to push a decision all the way down to the ground. China has known this for centuries. There is a classic idea that the emperor's power stopped at the county line. So the center sets the rule, and local practice fills in the rest.

The second condition is difference.for a country's regions that are  wildly unequal, A single national standard is either too heavy for the poor provinces or too small for the rich ones. So the center sets only a floor, and then lets each locality decide the real number. That is exactly what the pension map showed. Shanghai at 1490, and most provinces stuck at 143. The variation is not an accident. It is built into the design.

The third condition is money. The right is written at the center, but the funding sits with local government. And nothing in the law forces the center to cover a local shortfall. So the right only works when the locality can afford it. The law sets a ceiling, not a floor. And when the local money disappears, the right quietly disappears with it.

---

### [Slide 5a — China, the Story the State Tells]

Now let me show all three working together, in China, in a single year.

Start with the story the state tells. In 2024, Chinese state media ran a campaign around one phrase. The slash line, in Chinese zhǎnshā xiàn. The image was that in America, one accident or one illness can wipe a family out. Beijing Daily and Southern Daily pushed it as a trending topic on Weibo. Then the Party journal Qiushi turned it into official language. And the message was simple. China has the Social Insurance Law. It has Article 45 of the constitution. So China will not let anyone be slashed. Notice the move here. The law exists, and just by existing, it is doing all the work. No delivery required.

---

### [Slide 5b — China, What Actually Happened]

But now look at what the same year's audit actually found. 40.6 billion yuan moved out of the resident pension fund, across 13 provinces, and spent on salaries, on operations, and on debt. The central law had set the right. The local governments quietly moved the money. And no statute changed while they did it.

So by Pierson's method, nothing happened. The law still looks untouched. This is the heart of it. Welfare was cut, but the cut is invisible if all you read is the law.

---

### [Slide 5c — China, Why It Happened]

So why did the money run out. This is where the three conditions turn into a real story, and it starts back in 1994.

In 1994, China reformed its tax system. After that, the central government took the larger share of the revenue, while local governments kept the larger share of the spending duties. That left a gap. And local governments filled the gap by lending land. For about two decades, it worked. Land revenue became their main source of government income. At its peak, it was close to 30 percent of all government revenue.

Then the property market turned. _[Show Chart 2 — land revenue]_ Land revenue fell 16 percent in 2024 alone, and it is now down about 44 percent from its 2021 peak. So the cash that local budgets had leaned on for twenty years simply drained away. And once it drained, the local governments could not cover their basic duties. They could not pay all the salaries. They could not keep every office running. The Chinese term for these basic duties is the three guarantees. So in order to protect the three guarantees, they reached into the one large pot of money they still controlled. The pension fund.


---

### [Slide 6 — Beyond China]

Is it a China story? no and exactly the opposite. The same structure shows up across very different political systems, and usually more than one condition at a time. So let me give you three examples.

Take India. India is huge and federal, so the reach problem and the difference problem are both built in. The center legislates, but the states deliver, and they deliver unevenly. On top of that, the Indian constitution promises livelihood and social support, and then Article 37 says those promises cannot be enforced by any court. So there, the motive is written straight into the text. The right is real, and the obligation is removed.

Take Brazil. Brazil's 1988 constitution lists social rights in great detail. Health, food, housing, education. But the money problem and the difference problem decide what you actually receive. The benefit depends on what each city can pay. So the same right gives you one outcome in São Paulo, and a completely different one in poor, rural Maranhão.

Take South Africa. Its constitution guarantees housing and healthcare. But in the Grootboom case in 2000, the court ruled that the state only has to take reasonable steps within available resources. So the money condition and the discretion motive are written right into the law itself. And who decides what counts as reasonable? The state does.


---

### [Slide 7 — What This Means for Research]

So what does all this mean for how we study welfare. My argument fits in one line. We should measure what states do, not just what they write.

This changes two things. First, how we classify systems. Esping-Andersen's method reads the rules on paper, and so it overstates how protective these systems really are. On paper, China, India, Brazil and South Africa can look close to rich democracies. On the ground, they are not.

Second, how we measure cuts. Pierson only sees a cut when a law changes. But that misses what I call invisible retrenchment. When budgets shrink and benefits quietly fail to keep up, that is a real cut, even though no law has moved. And invisible retrenchment is still retrenchment. So the thing we measure cannot be the legal text alone.

---

### [Slide 8 — Close]

Let me come back to where I started. The state said, China's system will not let anyone be slashed. And here is my answer. That promise is only true while the local government can still afford it. Once the money runs out, a written right cannot enforce itself. And that is the legalization trap.

So I will leave you with three takeaways. First, a law can hand a state legitimacy without ever requiring it to deliver. Second, welfare can be cut without touching a single statute. And third, if we want to understand welfare honestly, we have to measure both the law and the machinery that is supposed to make the law real.

As populations age and budgets tighten almost everywhere, this reaches far beyond China. Writing the right is the easy part. Making it survive a fiscal shock is the real test.
